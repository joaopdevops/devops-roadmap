## 🇬🇧 English

**Hexadecimal: from "I can't do this" to converting a byte in 25 minutes**

The hex exercise left open on 06/09 was still pending. A written explanation prepared for it went unread — which repeats the Day 50 finding: **written material for later doesn't get read; live explanation anchors.** So it was re-taught live, with a new analogy.

**The analogy — a wallet with four bills:** R$8, R$4, R$2, R$1. Each bit says whether that bill is in the wallet (1) or not (0). Four bits can hold from 0 to 15, and hex writes that value in **one character**: 0–9, then A=10 … F=15.

| Input | Split | Value | Hex |
|---|---|---|---|
| `1111` | — | 8+4+2+1 = 15 | **F** ✅ |
| `11001010` | `1100` \| `1010` | 12 \| 10 | **CA** ✅ |

**Rule:** 1 hex digit = 4 bits, always. A byte is two 4-bit groups → two hex digits.

**Subnetting drill — /27:** method right (block size 32, mask = 256 − block), arithmetic slip: wrote 124, correct is **255.255.255.224**. 30 usable hosts ✅. Sanity check adopted: any mask from /25 up starts at 128 or higher.

**Teach-back in English (interview-style):** *"Can you explain what hexadecimal is and why network engineers use it?"* Got the definition and the 8-4-2-1 weights; missed the core (**4 bits per digit**) and inverted the *why* — MAC and IPv6 use hex **because** it's shorter and maps cleanly onto bits.

**Why it matters for DevOps:** MAC addresses, IPv6, memory addresses, hashes, container IDs and color codes are all hex. Reading `00:1A:2B…` as bytes instead of noise is baseline for network troubleshooting.
